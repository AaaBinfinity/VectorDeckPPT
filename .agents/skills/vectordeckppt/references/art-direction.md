## Aesthetic objective

Do not treat aesthetics as decoration added after layout. Use visual choices to make the argument easier to understand, remember, and trust. A successful deck should feel authored for this subject, audience, and setting rather than assembled from a generic presentation template.

Aim for four qualities at once:

- **clarity**: the eye finds the main claim and evidence without effort;
- **character**: the deck has a recognizable point of view without becoming theatrical;
- **coherence**: typography, color, imagery, geometry, and motion of attention belong to one system;
- **fitness**: the visual language matches the audience, subject matter, evidence, and delivery environment.

Polish is not the same as beauty. Beauty comes from proportion, hierarchy, restraint, tension, rhythm, and meaningful detail. Decorative complexity without communicative purpose is noise.

## Visual thesis

Before selecting colors or drawing components, write one visual thesis:

> This presentation should feel **[three precise qualities]**, because **[audience and subject]**, expressed through **[typography, spatial behavior, imagery, geometry, and color strategy]**.

Good qualities are specific enough to guide choices: editorial, exacting, civic, cinematic, tactile, optimistic, sober, archival, engineered, humane, restrained, kinetic, or monumental.

Avoid empty labels such as “modern,” “professional,” “high-end,” or “technology style” unless they are translated into visible decisions. For example:

```text
Weak: Modern technology style.

Useful: Engineered and calm; dense evidence sits on a strict editorial grid,
large numeric typography carries the argument, cobalt is reserved for causal
links, and diagrams use thin orthogonal lines with almost no decorative cards.
```

The thesis is a decision filter. Reject any font, color, image, or shape treatment that does not strengthen it.

## Meaning-to-form decisions

Translate communication intent into form before choosing a layout:

| Communication need | Favor | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Establish authority | disciplined grid, precise type, restrained palette, sourced evidence | ornamental badges, vague slogans, excessive gradients |
| Create urgency | large scale contrast, compressed sequence, decisive accent, visible consequence | alarm-red everywhere, crowded dashboards |
| Explain a system | clear topology, spatial grouping, consistent connector grammar | disconnected card inventories, decorative arrows |
| Compare alternatives | shared baseline, matched measures, controlled asymmetry | unrelated panels, unequal scales, remote legends |
| Make a human story memorable | documentary image, quotation, deliberate quiet, intimate scale | generic stock photos, icon substitutions for people |
| Signal transformation | before/after contrast, directional movement, changed density or light | a generic chevron process with no meaningful difference |
| Support a decision | implication-first title, evidence hierarchy, explicit recommendation | chronology, activity lists, neutral section labels |

Ask of every visible element: **what meaning does this form carry?** If the answer is only “it fills space,” remove or redesign it.

## Visual-source confirmation

Do not silently choose a style. Before source synthesis begins, confirm one visual-source route in the request contract:

- a user-selected bundled family;
- a user-supplied PPT/PPTX/PDF/image/screenshot/brand reference;
- a named hybrid of at most two sources; or
- explicit delegation for the Agent to select.

When no reference is supplied, recommend three bundled families based on audience, setting, evidence type, and desired emotional tone. State the benefit and tradeoff of each, then let the user choose, upload a reference, or delegate. When a reference is supplied, summarize the reusable visual grammar—type personality, spatial rhythm, crop behavior, color roles, material cues, and pacing—plus font, asset, rights, and editability constraints. Do not copy protected characters, logos, watermarks, proprietary artwork, or unlicensed fonts. A screenshot is evidence of desired behavior, not a full-slide background asset.

## Setting and credibility gate

Choose expressive intensity only after the complete request contract is confirmed. For executive, board, client, technical, academic, financial, legal, medical, public-sector, investor, audit, compliance, or other credibility-sensitive presentations, default to professional restraint: readable type, disciplined color, explicit sources, and evidence that dominates decoration.

Professional does not mean every object must sit in a horizontal or vertical card grid. Use editorial asymmetry, controlled overlap, cropped fields, diagonal or curved movement, scale contrast, off-axis captions, and a few deliberate grid breaks to create authorship without weakening trust. Let one hero typographic moment carry the proposition while ordinary evidence pages remain calm and legible.

Highly expressive comic, poster, collage, gaming, or campaign treatments are optional. Offer or use them when the setting benefits from memorability and cultural energy—such as launches, events, public campaigns, creative pitches, safety awareness, or youth-facing storytelling—and only after the user explicitly approves the direction. A reference image permits extraction of abstract behaviors such as contrast, cropping, rhythm, texture, typography, and geometry; it does not permit copying protected characters, logos, distinctive costumes, or identifying artwork.

## Direction families

Choose or hybridize a direction based on the material. These are starting logics, not templates.

### Editorial intelligence

Use for strategy, research, thought leadership, institutional narratives, and complex arguments.

- typography leads; images and data behave like evidence in a magazine feature;
- use strong columns, asymmetrical whitespace, pull quotes, rules, captions, and numbered sequences;
- favor a limited palette with one confident accent;
- vary scale and page density to create reading rhythm.

### Engineered systems

Use for architecture, infrastructure, technical programs, operations, and product mechanisms.

- reveal interfaces, dependencies, flow, and constraints;
- use a rigorous grid, exact labels, purposeful line grammar, and modular but non-card-heavy groupings;
- let system diagrams dominate when topology is the message;
- avoid “futuristic” decoration that obscures technical truth.

### Premium restraint

Use for executive narratives, premium products, brand launches, and concise high-stakes pitches.

- rely on exceptional typography, controlled contrast, generous negative space, and one memorable visual gesture;
- prefer fewer, larger elements and highly selective evidence;
- use material cues sparingly: a deep field, a fine rule, a precise crop, or one luminous accent;
- do not imitate luxury with black-and-gold clichés or gratuitous serif italics.

### Human documentary

Use for social impact, culture, healthcare, education, customer stories, and field research.

- make people, places, and lived detail visible;
- use authentic photography, verbatim evidence, warm neutral color, and readable annotation;
- let quiet pages create respect and attention;
- avoid sentimental stock imagery or flattening people into decorative portraits.

### Data-forward clarity

Use for analytical readouts, operating reviews, scientific evidence, and performance narratives.

- lead with the conclusion and design the chart around the comparison that proves it;
- use direct labels, stable scales, restrained color, annotations, and visible units;
- alternate overview and detail so the deck does not become one continuous dashboard;
- never use chart decoration as a substitute for analytical integrity.

### Expressive cultural

Use for creative work, events, media, fashion, art, and youth-facing narratives when the context permits stronger personality.

- build a distinctive typographic voice, bold cropping, controlled layering, and rhythmic color fields;
- use repetition with intentional breaks to create energy;
- keep the content hierarchy legible even when composition is unconventional;
- avoid random collage, trend imitation, or visual effects unrelated to the subject.

### Dynamic hero editorial

Use for product or brand launches, campaign storytelling, event openings, creative proposals, entertainment, games, and selected professional presentations that explicitly permit a stronger authored voice.

- build energy with near-black or paper fields, scarce signal red, hard white type, one cool micro-accent, slanted frames, crop tension, halftone rhythm, and abstract hero graphics;
- make a short `deck-title` or `section-title` the dominant image through scale, width, stacking, overlap, outline-like contrast, or controlled rotation;
- keep ordinary `slide-title` and body roles stable so evidence remains readable after the hero moment;
- use diagonals, arcs, off-grid labels, oversized numbers, and foreground/background overlap as compositional forces rather than decoration;
- avoid protected characters, copied logos or costume motifs, fake urgency metrics, illegible novelty fonts, red on every object, and using the direction in a credibility-sensitive setting without explicit approval.

## Composition, rhythm, and pacing

Think of the deck as a sequence of visual beats rather than independent canvases.

- Open with a clear world: one image, one proposition, or one strong typographic field.
- Build evidence with alternating density: statement → explanation → proof → synthesis.
- Use one dominant visual mass per slide. Supporting elements should form a clear second and third level.
- Create tension through controlled asymmetry, scale contrast, cropping, or a deliberate edge—not through random misalignment.
- Preserve recurring rails and spacing so varied silhouettes still feel related.
- Reserve the most dramatic scale or color contrast for the most important moment.
- Let section transitions change pace without abandoning the visual thesis.
- Resolve the close visually: simplify, synthesize, or return to an opening motif with new meaning.

Use negative space as an active shape. It can isolate a claim, direct attention, imply confidence, or create a pause. Empty space becomes accidental only when the remaining elements lack a clear relationship.

## Typography as image

Typography is the main visual material in most presentations.

- Choose a type personality that supports the visual thesis before choosing decorative motifs.
- Use scale, weight, width, case, line breaks, and alignment to create voice.
- Protect meaningful line endings. Rewrite a title to achieve a stronger silhouette rather than accepting an awkward wrap.
- Pair Chinese and Latin text by perceived weight and x-height, not merely by nominal font size.
- Use large numerals, short quotations, or a key phrase as a visual object only when they carry the slide's meaning.
- Keep body copy typographically quiet. A deck cannot have five simultaneous voices.
- Prefer one family with a disciplined weight range; introduce a second family only when the contrast has a defined role.

## Color, material, and depth

Assign color roles rather than selecting a collection of attractive swatches:

```text
field / surface / primary text / secondary text / structural line /
accent / semantic positive / semantic warning / semantic negative
```

Decide the deck's light behavior: bright editorial paper, deep cinematic field, warm tactile surface, or neutral technical canvas. Keep it consistent.

Use depth only to express hierarchy or spatial relationship. Prefer overlap, scale, tonal separation, and border contrast before using shadows. If shadows are used, define one restrained shadow language and apply it consistently. Avoid glassmorphism, neon glows, and gradients unless they are central to the visual thesis and acceptable as SVG fallback.

## Imagery and illustration briefs

Do not request or select an image with only a noun. Write an art-directed asset brief:

```text
Role in slide:
Subject and action:
Point of view / camera distance:
Composition and negative-space requirement:
Lighting and atmosphere:
Palette relationship:
Authenticity or factual constraints:
What must not appear:
Crop and aspect ratio:
```

Example:

```text
Role: establish the scale and human stakes of the inspection workflow.
Subject: one field technician examining a rail joint, not posing for camera.
View: medium-wide, low eye level, environment still legible.
Composition: subject in right third; quiet dark space on left for title.
Light: cool dawn with one warm practical light; documentary, not cinematic haze.
Palette: slate, steel, muted amber; compatible with cobalt annotations.
Exclude: holograms, floating UI, safety violations, generic office setting.
Crop: 16:9 cover with head and hands protected.
```

When no suitable image is available, use typography, data, diagrams, or abstract geometry grounded in the subject. Do not insert an irrelevant stock image merely to make a slide feel “visual.”

## Art-direction brief

Before authoring slides, complete this internal brief:

```text
Communication job:
Audience and setting:
Emotional trajectory:
Visual thesis:
Direction family and why:
Primary visual material (type / data / photography / diagrams / product):
Grid and spatial behavior:
Typography voice:
Color and light behavior:
Image crop and treatment:
Shape / line / icon grammar:
Density and pacing plan:
Opening gesture:
Peak visual moment:
Closing resolution:
Three clichés to avoid:
```

Then challenge it:

1. Could this direction belong to any unrelated company or subject? If yes, make it more specific.
2. Does the direction help the audience understand and trust the content? If no, simplify it.
3. Can the supported SVG and PPTX mapping reproduce it faithfully? If no, adapt the concept before authoring.
4. Is there enough range for the full deck without switching styles? If no, define composition families within the same system.

## Aesthetic anti-patterns

- Defaulting to blue-purple gradients, glowing blobs, circuit lines, or “AI” imagery for every technology topic.
- Repeating a centered title and three equal cards regardless of content.
- Making every composition orthogonal, evenly boxed, and visually interchangeable in the name of professionalism.
- Using large rounded containers as the universal answer to grouping.
- Treating icons as evidence or replacing specific concepts with generic symbols.
- Filling negative space with rings, dots, waves, or grids that carry no meaning.
- Making every page equally dense, equally bright, and equally emphatic.
- Using tiny captions, weak gray text, or decorative typography that fails at projection distance.
- Combining unrelated visual trends—glass, brutalism, 3D, gradients, editorial serif—without one thesis.
- Choosing stock imagery for superficial demographic representation rather than authentic relevance.
- Copying the source document's hierarchy instead of designing a presentation hierarchy.

## Approval questions

Before locking the direction, answer yes to all:

- Is the aesthetic idea expressed in observable design decisions?
- Does it fit the audience, setting, content, and credibility requirement?
- Has the user explicitly approved any expressive treatment that departs from the professional default?
- Does each proposed visual device have a communication role?
- Can typography carry the deck even when imagery is absent?
- Can multiple slide silhouettes emerge without changing the visual language?
- Does the direction avoid obvious presentation and generative-AI clichés?
- Can it be authored within the supported SVG subset with acceptable editability?
- Will the deck still look intentional when rendered in PowerPoint rather than only as SVG?
